# Keep the write-up short, then let judges break the arm

Your current draft approach is mostly right: a plain-language write-up of about 700 words, no equations, and one headline timing number sits squarely inside the range of real winning hardware write-ups. Thirteen winners scraped from Hack the North, nwHacks and MakeUofT ran **418 to 955 words (median about 560)**, and **none used tables, code blocks or equations** ([QNX-RasPi-Setup-Util](https://devpost.com/software/qnx-raspi-setup-util), [DUM-E](https://devpost.com/software/dum-e-kgx6at), [Spyder](https://devpost.com/software/spyder)). What the draft is missing is not length or maths but scannable evidence: a hero photo and demo video at the top, one labelled paragraph per sponsor prize that names exactly what you used, an architecture picture, a candid Challenges section, and a clear line between what works and what is planned. The live pitch matters more than the text, because judges spend only **2 to 5 minutes per project** and most of it at your table ([MLH Judging Plan](https://guide.mlh.com/general-information/judging-and-submissions/judging-plan.md)). Spend about two-thirds of your 3 minutes showing the arm, and make your reliability claims visible by letting the judge crash a process while the gripper keeps holding. One caution up front: the research notes found nothing on the Tiger Data, Vultr or PCBWay prize criteria, or on Hack Western 13's own rubric, so check those on the event Devpost before you finalize.

## Your 700-word plan matches winners, with five fixes

Judges skim. A judge who reads only your first two paragraphs should already know the problem, who it is for, and how it works ([DoraHacks](https://dev.to/dorahacks/what-does-a-good-hackathon-submission-look-like-5398)). One judge who scored a year of events in 2026 found the strongest submissions opened with a plain sentence of the form **"this is a tool that does X for Y"**, and that narrow, finished projects beat broad ones ([Kurbaitaev](https://dev.to/kurbaitaev/what-judges-actually-score-notes-from-a-year-of-hackathon-judging-3p4l)). So keep it short and plain. Keep Devpost's template headings too; teams that delete them tend to drop key details ([Biggin](https://dev.to/jacklynbiggin/how-to-make-your-devpost-submissions-not-suck-37i0)).

Here is what to change. **First, put media before words.** 11 of the 13 sampled winners embedded a YouTube demo, and every one had a photo gallery. Use a well-lit photo of the assembled arm as the cover (not a breadboard or CAD render), plus build photos and a legible wiring diagram ([Position Is Everything](https://www.positioniseverything.net/how-to-document-and-demo-a-hardware-hackathon-project-so-judges-can-rebuild-it/)). A short GIF of the arm moving is a natural gallery item. **Second, add an architecture diagram as an image.** QNX judges explicitly praised architecture documentation in past winners ([QNX blog: cuHacking 6](https://qnx.software/en/blog/2026/qnx-at-cuhacking-6)), and a picture of your processes (vision, classifier, gripper control, supervisor, e-stop) carries the technical depth without adding words. **Third, make "How we built it" your longest section.** In hardware winners it ran 235 to 388 words, often split into Hardware and Software subheads, and named the exact parts ([ROSS](https://devpost.com/software/ross-42pnvi), [Spyder](https://devpost.com/software/spyder)). **Fourth, write Challenges honestly and concretely.** Winners told stories like "we fried our EMG sensor, twice!" ([PulseGrip](https://devpost.com/software/pulsegrip)) and servo power brownouts ([Quicker Picker Upper](https://devpost.com/software/the-quicker-picker-upper)); naming a known weakness before being asked raised scores ([Kurbaitaev](https://dev.to/kurbaitaev/what-judges-actually-score-notes-from-a-year-of-hackathon-judging-3p4l)). **Fifth, separate working from planned**, and never describe a planned feature in the present tense ([Position Is Everything](https://www.positioniseverything.net/how-to-document-and-demo-a-hardware-hackathon-project-so-judges-can-rebuild-it/)).

On the "one number" rule: keep it, but state it as a comparison with how you measured it. Your worst-case gripper-loop lateness of **0.49 ms at real-time priority versus 137 ms at normal priority under CPU load** is exactly the kind of figure a real-time sponsor wants, and almost no winners include measured numbers at all. A single tiny table (for example, latency per pipeline stage) would probably be fine, but no source tested whether tables help, so leave it out unless it adds something the sentence cannot. Equations belong in the repo, not the write-up. Never include a number you did not measure.

## One story, with a labelled paragraph per prize

The risk with five sponsor targets is a diluted story. The fix is one main story (who struggles to grasp things, and the arm you point at to help them) and then short labelled paragraphs, one per prize, each saying **which product you used, why, and what it does in your project**. Sponsor judges search for their product and give up if it is buried ([Biggin](https://dev.to/jacklynbiggin/how-to-make-your-devpost-submissions-not-suck-37i0); [DoraHacks](https://dev.to/dorahacks/what-does-a-good-hackathon-submission-look-like-5398)). Many winners took two or three prizes with one coherent project, such as Spyder (finalist plus Viam) and RoBotany (finalist plus BentoML) ([HTN 2023 gallery](https://hackthenorth2023.devpost.com/project-gallery)).

**Best Use of QNX.** Past QNX winners were rewarded for real use of RTOS features such as thread priorities and multithreading, for solving problems without sample code, and for end-to-end completeness ([QNX blog: Hack the 6ix 2025](https://qnx.software/en/blog/2026/hack-the-6ix-2025)). Both 2025 Hack the North QNX winners were plain, roughly 450 to 500 word write-ups, so the bar is clear evidence of real QNX use rather than polish ([QNXMobility](https://devpost.com/software/canqnx)). Use QNX's own vocabulary: "spatial and temporal isolation", "deterministic behavior", "microkernel" and "priority-based scheduling" ([QNX OS for Safety 8.0](https://qnx.software/en/software/products-and-solutions/qnx-os-and-os-for-safety)), and its current theme, "physical AI" ([QNX blog](https://qnx.software/en/blog/2026/how-qnx-gives-developers-the-building-blocks-for-physical-ai)). For the required open-source module, name the QNX-ported **TensorFlow Lite `tflite_runtime`** Python package ([DeepWiki mirror of qnx-ports](https://deepwiki.com/qnx-ports/build-files/6.2-tensorflow-lite)). QNX's own MediaPipe port is C++ only and still "in-flight", so getting the hand tracker running in Python on QNX 8.0 is a genuine contribution worth saying out loud. Before submitting, confirm the exact package name and version on the Pi (for example with `pip show`), because a judge may ask and the research could not confirm the listing on oss.qnx.com.

**Sun Life Best Health Hack.** Lead with a sourced, specific user. Statistics Canada reports that **more than 1.5 million Canadians (5.0%) have a dexterity disability**, rising to **11.3% at age 65 and over**, and **28% have unmet support needs because of cost** ([StatCan](https://www150.statcan.gc.ca/n1/pub/11-627-m/11-627-m2024054-eng.htm)). Verify these on the StatCan page, because the researcher could only read a summary. Commercial assistive arms like the Kinova JACO cost around **USD 35,000 to 50,000** ([Futurism](https://futurism.com/the-byte/robot-arm-wheelchair)), so a low-cost, fail-safe helper speaks to a real cost gap. Frame the crash recovery and the force-controlled grip as patient safety: a user who cannot catch a dropped hot mug needs the arm to keep holding. Say "designed for", not "proven for", and do not claim certification. QNX OS for Safety is certified to IEC 62304 Class C (medical device software), but the Pi image is standard SDP 8.0, so the honest line is "the same RTOS family that's certified for medical devices."

**PCBWay, Tiger Data and Vultr.** The notes contain no research on these prizes' criteria. For PCBWay Best Hardware Prototype, the hardware documentation advice above (cover photo of the real device, build photos, wiring diagram, a parts list with exact models) is the best available guide ([Position Is Everything](https://www.positioniseverything.net/how-to-document-and-demo-a-hardware-hackathon-project-so-judges-can-rebuild-it/)). For Tiger Data and Vultr, only enter if you genuinely use their product, and give each its own labelled paragraph saying what it stores or hosts. A shallow add-on usually reads as one; sponsor advice is to "focus on deep integrations" ([Cloudinary](https://dev.to/cloudinary/how-to-win-a-hackathon-1377)).

Finally, sound like yourselves. Judges view vagueness about what was built at the event as "hiding" ([Kurbaitaev](https://dev.to/kurbaitaev/what-judges-actually-score-notes-from-a-year-of-hackathon-judging-3p4l)), and stronger submissions said plainly how AI tools helped (boilerplate, debugging, documentation) ([Singh](https://dev.to/amising6/what-i-learned-after-reviewing-many-ai-and-developer-projects-as-a-hackathon-judge-2g06)). Cut stock phrases like "seamless" or "revolutionize", and keep the messy specifics only your team knows: the part that burned out, the value you tuned, the hour it took.

## Spend two minutes of the pitch showing the arm

Judges report that the score is largely set in the first 30 seconds, so rehearse that part word for word ([Kurbaitaev](https://dev.to/kurbaitaev/what-judges-actually-score-notes-from-a-year-of-hackathon-judging-3p4l)). Guides recommend problem and solution in about 15 to 20 seconds each, then a live demo of 60 to 90 seconds or more, and practising at least three times with a timer ([Builderbase](https://intercom.help/builderbase/en/articles/14836128-builder-s-guide-ch-14-crafting-a-demo-that-lands)). A working end-to-end demo beats a more ambitious project told as a story, and "the one wow moment has to be real" ([John Kim](https://getpushtoprod.substack.com/p/how-to-win-hackathons)). A suggested script:

| Time | What you do | What it scores |
|---|---|---|
| 0:00–0:25 | "One in ten Canadian seniors struggles to grasp small objects. We built an arm you control by pointing, running on QNX on a Raspberry Pi. Let me show you." | Innovation, health story |
| 0:25–1:15 | Judge points at an object; the classifier names it; the arm picks it with a gentle, force-controlled grip. | Completeness, Design |
| 1:15–2:15 | The "break it" moment: the judge kills the vision process (or presses a button). The supervisor restarts it on screen and the arm keeps holding. Then hit the e-stop. | Technical Complexity, QNX |
| 2:15–2:45 | One technical sentence (0.49 ms vs 137 ms under load) and one honest limitation (for example, lighting). | Technical, credibility |
| 2:45–3:00 | Repeat the one-line identity. | Memorability |

The middle segment is your strongest asset. Abstract properties score only when judges see them, and QNX documents its High Availability Manager as a "smart watchdog" that restarts processes, with `slay` able to kill a process by name ([QNX HAM docs](https://qnx.com/developers/docs/8.0/com.qnx.doc.neutrino.prog/topic/process_High_Availability.html); [QNX slay](https://qnx.com/developers/docs/8.0/com.qnx.doc.neutrino.utilities/topic/s/slay.html)). Letting the judge cause the failure makes it memorable and tactile, which showcase guides favour ([UCI BB Comp 2025](https://bbcomp.tech.uci.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/BB-Comp-Showcase-Tips-and-Tricks-2025.pdf)). Put a big on-screen counter next to it, such as "restarts: 1, arm holding". If the judge's badge or a quick question tells you their track, keep the demo identical and change only the opening hook and where the extra 20 seconds go: QNX judges get the crash and timing moment, health judges get the user and the fail-safe.

Because science-fair judging stacks each judge's top three picks ([MLH Judging Plan](https://guide.mlh.com/general-information/judging-and-submissions/judging-plan.md)), the goal is to be someone's most memorable project, not merely solid on every criterion.

## Rehearse the failure, not just the success

You will run the same demo many times over a couple of hours, so reliability is half the job. Freeze the code early and only demo what has worked **three times in a row**; assign a "Talker" and a "Fixer"; check batteries before each judge; and assume Wi-Fi will fail ([Holson, General Robots](https://generalrobots.substack.com/p/nailing-the-demo)). Build a one-command "reset to known state" between judges. Keep a 60-second recording of a good run one tap away ([Kurbaitaev](https://dev.to/kurbaitaev/what-judges-actually-score-notes-from-a-year-of-hackathon-judging-3p4l)). If something breaks, the Talker says a pre-written line ("Hardware being hardware, here's this morning's run while we reset"), and nobody opens a terminal to debug in front of the judge ([Builderbase](https://intercom.help/builderbase/en/articles/14836128-builder-s-guide-ch-14-crafting-a-demo-that-lands)). Rehearse the crash-recovery moment as part of the three-in-a-row test, because a recovery demo that fails is worse than none.

For the 1-minute Q&A, aim for about three answers of one or two sentences each. Pre-write answers to "Why QNX instead of Linux?" (isolation, determinism, restart, all just shown), "What runs on the Pi versus your laptop?" (be exact), "What breaks first?" (one real weakness), "Who is the user, and is it safe?", and "What did you build this weekend versus before?" Claiming nothing breaks costs credibility; naming a specific weakness raises scores ([Kurbaitaev](https://dev.to/kurbaitaev/what-judges-actually-score-notes-from-a-year-of-hackathon-judging-3p4l)). "We haven't figured that out yet, but our hypothesis is..." is an acceptable answer ([Builderbase](https://intercom.help/builderbase/en/articles/14836128-builder-s-guide-ch-14-crafting-a-demo-that-lands)).

## Conclusion

The write-up and the pitch do different jobs. The write-up serves the judges who are not standing at your table: the sponsor engineer checking eligibility in 30 seconds, the shortlist, and the post-event check. So it should be short, skimmable, honest and labelled. The pitch is where you win, and your project has something most hackathon arms lack: a failure the judge can cause and watch the system survive. Build both around that moment. Show the crash recovery live, back it with the one measured number, and connect it to the person who cannot catch a dropped mug, and the same 3 minutes covers QNX, health and technical complexity at once.
